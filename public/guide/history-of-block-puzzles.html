A short history of block puzzles

Fitting shapes into a grid is one of the oldest puzzle ideas there is. Here is how it went from a mathematician's notebook to one of the most played game genres in the world.

Polyominoes, 1950s

In 1953 the American mathematician Solomon Golomb named and studied "polyominoes": shapes made of squares joined edge to edge. The twelve pentominoes, made of five squares each, became a classic recreational puzzle, popularised in the 1950s and 1960s through puzzle columns and books. Almost every piece you place in a modern block puzzle is a polyomino.

Tetris, 1984

Soviet engineer Alexey Pajitnov created Tetris in 1984, using four-square pieces (tetrominoes) that fall into a well. Clearing full rows to make room became one of the most recognisable mechanics in video game history.

Grid puzzles without gravity, 2010s

On smartphones, a new family appeared: no falling pieces, no timer, just a square grid and a few shapes to place wherever they fit. Full rows and columns disappear, and the game ends when nothing fits. 1010!, released in 2014, made the format hugely popular, and many games followed with wooden boards, sudoku-style regions or bigger grids.

Why these games work

They are easy to learn in seconds, they reward planning without demanding fast reflexes, and every clear gives a small, satisfying reward. A game can last one minute or thirty, which makes them perfect for a break.

The next step: playing against someone

Almost all of these games are solo: you against the board. Blockmate asks a different question: what if two players shared the same grid? Suddenly each piece you place also shapes your opponent's options, the pieces you skip become their hand, and you can win by leaving them no room. The puzzle becomes a strategy duel, closer to chess than to a high-score chase.

FAQ

What is a polyomino?

A shape made of squares joined edge to edge, like the pieces of a block puzzle.

Is Blockmate like Tetris?

Not quite: pieces don't fall and there is no rush. You place them freely on a shared 8×8 board, turn by turn, against an opponent.
